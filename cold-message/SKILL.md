# Cold message

A cold message has exactly one job: get a reply. Not a meeting, not a demo, not a click. A reply.
Everything below follows from that. The rules come from Lavender's study of 28.3 million sales emails
(shared by Nick Abraham, Oct 2026) and apply to any first-touch message: email, LinkedIn, X DM, Slack
community DM. Where a rule is email-only, it says so.

## Before writing

Ask for (or infer from the conversation) three things. If any are missing, ask once, briefly, then draft
with a clearly marked placeholder rather than stalling.

1. **Who** the recipient is: role, company, and one specific thing the sender knows about them
   (a post they wrote, a hire they made, a product launch, a job change, a number in their earnings call).
2. **What problem** the sender solves, in the recipient's terms, and one customer story that matches
   the recipient's size, industry, and problem.
3. **Channel**: email, LinkedIn, X DM, etc. This sets the length ceiling and whether a subject line exists.

If the sender's "research" is a guess, say so in the draft. Wrong facts are worse than no facts;
the reader feels the message was never really for them.

## The 20 rules

Treat these as defaults with reasons, not a checklist to recite. Deviate only when the user gives a reason.

### Length and readability

1. **Write 25–50 words.** Lavender found 65% more replies than a 125-word email. Aim here first.
2. **Never exceed 75 words.** Readers scan a message for about 11 seconds. Past 75 words the scan fails.
3. **Write at a 3rd–5th grade reading level.** Short words, short sentences. 67% more replies.
   No jargon, no "leverage", "synergy", "streamline", "robust", "end-to-end".
4. **Use 1–2 sentence paragraphs.** The first open is 8x more likely to happen on a phone.
   Every paragraph should fit on a phone screen without the thumb moving.

### Opening

5. **Lead with an observation about them.** First line proves the message was written for this
   person. Not "I noticed you're the VP of Sales" (that's their title, not an observation) but
   "Saw you just opened the Austin office" or "Your post on churn in PLG hit on something we see a lot."
6. **Turn the observation into a hypothesis.** Connect what you noticed to the problem you solve:
   "Opening a second office usually means onboarding reps remotely, which is where ramp time slips."
   A hypothesis invites correction, and correction is a reply.
7. **Double-check the research.** A wrong fact makes the whole message feel automated. If you cannot
   verify it, soften it to a question ("looks like..." / "guessing that...") or cut it.

### Body

8. **Focus on their problem, not on you.** The buyer does not care about the sender's company, funding,
   or feature list. Cut every sentence that starts with "We" unless it describes an outcome for someone like them.
9. **Use one relevant customer story.** Same size, same industry, same problem. "Helped a 40-rep
   fintech team cut ramp from 5 months to 3" beats a logo wall.
10. **Don't ask for the meeting.** The goal is a reply. Asking for 30 minutes on the first touch is
    asking a stranger for a favor. Earn the meeting in the second message.
11. **Make the question easy to answer.** Skip open-ended questions ("What are your priorities this
    quarter?"). Ask something answerable in one word or one line: "Is ramp time something you're
    looking at this year?" or "Worth a look, or wrong person?"
12. **Use hedge words.** "Might," "seems," "could," "guessing" lower the stakes of replying and
    leave room for the reader to correct you. Overconfidence reads as a pitch; a hedge reads as a conversation.
13. **Drop the "did you know" stats.** Informative or teaching tones destroy reply rates. The reader
    does not want to be educated by a stranger.
14. **Make one ask.** Multiple asks (reply AND book a call AND check the deck) are on Lavender's
    "not working" list. One message, one question.
15. **For executives, lead with outcomes.** Revenue, cost, or risk, in that order of likelihood.
    An exec does not care about features, workflows, or integrations.

### Subject line (email only)

16. **Keep subject lines to 1–3 words.** Boring is best. "ramp time", "austin office", "quick question" (if honest).
17. **Make it sound internal.** Like something a colleague would send. No verbs, no numbers, no
    questions, no first names, no title case, no emoji. "Q3 pipeline" not "Boost Your Q3 Pipeline by 40%!"
18. **Cut the clichés.** "I hope this finds you well" and "Hope you had a great weekend" waste the
    preview text, which is the second thing the reader sees after the subject line. The first line
    of the body should carry the observation (rule 5) so the preview earns the open.

### Deliverability and cadence (email only)

19. **No images, no links** in the first touch. Both hurt deliverability and trip spam filters.
    If a link is essential, offer it ("happy to send the write-up if useful") rather than include it.
20. **Send 50 or fewer cold emails a day per inbox, and space them out.** Bursts trigger spam
    filters. Tell the user this if they mention sequences, tools, or volume.

## Channel adjustments

- **LinkedIn connection note**: 300-character hard limit. One observation, one hedge, one easy
  question. No pitch at all; the pitch comes after they accept.
- **LinkedIn message / InMail, X DM, Slack DM**: No subject line. The first line IS the subject
  line, so it must be the observation. Keep under 50 words; DMs are read even faster than email.
- **Follow-up (no reply)**: Do not "bump" or "circle back". Add one new piece of value or a new
  hypothesis in under 40 words. Never guilt them ("I haven't heard back...").
- **Recruiting, investor, or partnership outreach**: Same rules. The "customer story" becomes the
  relevant proof point (a similar hire who thrived, a comparable company in the portfolio).

## Output format

Deliver the message ready to paste, then a short audit so the user can see the rules at work.

```
Subject: <1–3 words, internal-sounding>   (email only)

<observation about them, 1–2 sentences>

<hypothesis tying it to the problem, 1–2 sentences>

<one customer story or proof point, 1 sentence, optional>

<one easy-to-answer, hedged question>

<first name only, no title block, no signature>
```

Then, in two or three lines:
- Word count and reading grade (rules 1–3).
- Which fact the message depends on, so the sender can verify it before sending (rule 7).
- Anything the user asked for that you left out and why (a link, a stat, a meeting ask).

When asked to **critique** an existing message, score it against the 20 rules by number, quote the
exact phrase that breaks each rule, and then give a rewritten version in the same format.

## Example

Input: "Cold email to the VP of Customer Success at Loom. We do onboarding analytics. They just
posted about rebuilding their onboarding flow."

Output:

```
Subject: onboarding rebuild

Saw your post on rebuilding Loom's onboarding flow.

Guessing the hard part isn't the redesign, it's knowing which step actually loses people once it ships.

We helped Notion's CS team find their drop-off step in week one instead of quarter one.

Worth a look, or is that already covered?

Samir
```

Audit: 50 words, grade 4. Depends on the post about the onboarding rebuild being real and recent.
Left out: the product link and the "book a demo" ask, since both cut reply rates on a first touch.

## What to avoid, concretely

Cut these on sight, each one is a reply-rate killer in the data:
"I hope this finds you well", "I wanted to reach out", "My name is", "We're a leading", "quick 15 minutes",
"Did you know that", "Just following up", "circling back", "any thoughts?", "let me know", bullet lists
of features, more than one question mark, a signature block with phone number and logo.
